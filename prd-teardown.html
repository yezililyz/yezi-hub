PRD teardown · how & why it works
✦ Anatomy of a great PRD

A tiny feature, written like a strategic decision.

Linear's "priority micro-adjust" PRD ships something small — drag-and-drop reordering within a priority bucket. But the writing is a masterclass: it reframes a feature request as a problem, solves it in the most on-brand way possible, and says no to the powerful options on principle. This is a teardown — and a framework you can lift.

It resolves a feature request by reframing it as a problem — then solves the problem in the most opinionated, on-brand way possible. — the one-line takeaway
01

It leads with the problem, not the solution

Structure: Context → Problem → Solution

The PRD opens on the customer-facing one-liner, then spends its weight on the problem before proposing anything. It names three concrete failure modes of Linear's current manual-ordering system — so the solution later feels inevitable rather than invented.

1

Instability

Manual sort feels low-stakes, so users reorder casually — not realizing someone else relies on that order. The ranking can't be trusted.

2

Local vs. global confusion

In most apps manual sort is local; Linear's is global. Users are surprised, and act on a wrong mental model.

3

Redundancy with the priority field

Linear has both manual sort and a priority field. For a global stack-rank, it's awkward to have a priority field that disagrees with your sort.

Why this is good craft: a reader who only gets through the Problem section already understands why something must change. The solution becomes the obvious resolution of a tension the PRD carefully built — not a feature pitched in a vacuum.
02

It decodes indirect requests

Users describe solutions; PMs extract problems

The sharpest move in the document: customers never ask for "micro-adjust." They ask for adjacent solutions. The PRD explicitly translates those indirect feature requests back into the real underlying need — the hallmark of senior product thinking.

What they ask for

"Custom priority levels" — because the 4 basic buckets don't feel granular enough.

→
The real need

A finer, stable order within a level — without inventing and maintaining new categories.

What they ask for

"Custom fields" — to add a numeric "ranking" column for a manual stack-rank.

→
The real need

An opinionated, global priority order — expressed by direct manipulation, not arbitrary integers.

The lesson: when feature requests cluster around a theme, the requests are symptoms. The PRD's job is to name the disease. Treating "custom levels" and "custom fields" as one underlying need is what unlocks a single, elegant solution.
03

The opinionated solution

Small surface, big positioning

The solution is almost disarmingly simple: let users drag to rearrange relative priority within a bucket. The craft is in the framing — it's positioned as a behavior-recommendation engine, not just a feature.

Priority levels already exist as broad buckets (low, med, high, urgent). Rather than add structure, the PRD adds order inside the structure that's already there. And it commits to a stance: "we will strongly recommend that all stack-ranking use-cases be achieved through priority + micro-adjust." The feature is small; the intent is to consolidate a fragmented priority story into one system.

Why this is good: the smallest possible surface that fully resolves the problem — and a clear opinion about how users should work. Opinionated defaults are the product's identity, and the solution leans into it rather than hedging.
04

It says no on principle

Rejected options, with reasons

A great PRD shows its work on the roads not taken. This one rejects the two more "powerful" options — and ties each rejection to Linear's product identity, not just effort.

✕ Custom priority levels

Doesn't actually solve stack-ranking, and would tempt power users into creating far too many distinct categories — more complexity, less clarity.

✕ Custom fields

Would solve stack-ranking (and much more) — but it's out of character for Linear. Ease-of-use comes from opinionated defaults that work the same everywhere.

The tell: the custom-fields rejection turns down the most capable option because it would erode the product's core differentiator. Saying no to power in defense of identity is the single most senior move in the document.
05

It grounds claims in evidence

Real Slack, real mockups

Abstract personas are cheap. This PRD anchors its two usage scenarios in actual customer artifacts — a Slack thread and a user-made mockup — then shows how micro-adjust serves each without building what they literally requested.

User A — "give me more priority categories"

A real Slack exchange: the customer wants more granularity because grouping hundreds of projects under five categories hides specific priorities. The PM's reply previews the actual solution — re-arrange within a grouping by drag-and-drop. The customer's reaction: "ah awesome bcs of the manual part."

User B — "let me add an 'importance' integer field"

A customer mocked up ordering by a custom field they named "importance." Micro-adjust serves the same need by direct manipulation — "less mentally taxing than trying to think of an arbitrary integer or explaining what such a number means to the team."

Why this works: evidence converts opinion into observation. Showing the literal request and the better answer proves the PM understood the user more deeply than the user understood themselves.
06

Rollout planned as a story

Internal → Beta → GA

The milestones aren't a task dump. Each carries the one real concern at that stage — including the migration thinking junior PRDs skip — and the launch itself is a deliberate narrative choice.

MS1 · INTERNAL

Spec the hard edge case precisely

Drag within a priority sets fine-grained order. Drag between priorities: moving up takes the lower priority, moving down takes the higher. The exact ambiguity engineers would otherwise guess — pinned down.

MS2 · BETA

Change management: bootstrap from existing data

Initialize each user's micro-adjust order from their existing manual-sort index — because that was the recommended stack-ranking solution. No one starts from a blank slate.

MS3 · GA

Ship alongside project priorities, one changelog

Release micro-adjust and project-level priorities together "to tell a more cohesive story about the priority system." Even the announcement is a product decision.

Why this is good: the rollout reflects the same consolidation thesis as the feature — fragmented pieces brought into one coherent priority system, communicated as a single story at launch.
07

What it actually resolves

User & business problem

Strip away the craft and two problems are being solved at once — one for the user, one for the business. The elegance is that a single small feature addresses both.

User problem

Power users who stack-rank everything were forced into bad workarounds — abusing an unstable manual sort, or begging for custom fields and levels that add cognitive overhead ("what does importance = 7 mean?"). Micro-adjust lets them express relative priority by direct manipulation, which is far less mentally taxing.

Business problem

A fragmented priority story (manual sort vs. priority field — redundant and conflicting) plus feature-request pressure pushing Linear toward custom fields that would erode its core differentiator: opinionated simplicity. Micro-adjust absorbs the demand while reinforcing identity — and creates a defensible, unique integration of priority + ranking no competitor offers.

Prior art angle: the PRD notes no other tool offers manual adjustment within priorities — they all pick a rejected option. Integrating priority and stack-ranking into one system is the differentiation.
08

The extractable framework

Lift this into your own PRDs

Generalized from the teardown — eight moves you can apply to any PRD, whatever you're building.

1

Lead with the problem. Spend your weight naming failure modes before proposing anything. The solution should feel inevitable.

2

Decode indirect requests. When asks cluster, they're symptoms — name the underlying need behind "can you add X?"

3

Solve at the smallest surface. Add order to existing structure before adding new structure. Resist scope.

4

Show rejected options with reasons. Tie each "no" to principle and identity, not just effort.

5

Ground scenarios in real evidence. Slack threads, mockups, quotes — observation beats opinion.

6

Spec the hard edge case. Pin down the one ambiguity engineers would otherwise guess.

7

Plan migration explicitly. Bootstrap users from existing data; never strand them on a blank slate.

8

Launch as a story. Sequence and bundle the announcement so the feature reinforces a larger, coherent product narrative.

The meta-lesson: a great PRD isn't long — it's opinionated. Every section makes a defensible choice and shows why. Reframe the request, solve it on-brand, and say no to everything that dilutes the product's identity.
